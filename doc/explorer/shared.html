How many people are Downtown at 08:00? Who might visit a new shop? Both questions start with a population and a day of activities. They require different observations to check the answer.

From a population to a weekday

Each city constructs a synthetic population from local evidence. A resident has a stable identifier, an activity diary, and locations for activities such as home, work or shopping. Incoming workers form a separate cohort. The city determines how people acquire diaries, workplaces and destinations. The shared simulator takes the resulting schedules and counts where people spend time and which areas they travel between.

  1. Prepare the run. A versioned manifest identifies the geography, population, weekday and available scenarios. Datahike indexes those records. The engine prepares the schedules as arrays before evaluating the day.
  2. Evaluate the clock. Each schedule supplies a sequence of time intervals. A person contributes to one area or state in each minute. Travelling, outside the modeled area, and unresolved locations have distinct meanings wherever the release supplies them.
  3. Count trips. A departure contributes to the origin at departure time. An arrival contributes to the destination at arrival time. A trip leaving at 07:55 and arriving at 08:10 therefore appears in two different hourly totals.

What a dot represents

For area a and minute t, presence is the sum of the people whose schedule puts them in a at t. Including the travel and unresolved states, these counts must add back to the modeled cohort. The explorer calculates area overlays for the full run. Its display limit controls how many people it draws.

A moving dot follows a sampled trip with a street path and timestamps. In roster view, a grey dot marks a person’s last modeled stop until the next known arrival. Selecting a traveller shows speed on the simulation clock. Each city page explains which trips have street paths and what evidence sets their speeds.

How to read a scenario

A scenario compares two outcomes under a specified change. For a shop, the reported effect is the scenario outcome minus its baseline. A visit is counted in people or trips. Spending also requires a monetary model, a currency, and a time period. Stuttgart estimates annual resident retail spending by district and demand class. Vancouver’s East Hastings example follows weekday grocery arrivals on foot and applies a basket chosen in the explorer.

What can be checked?

A control is a quantity the model is constructed or fitted to reproduce, such as a census population total. A held-out observation tests a prediction using data kept out of that fit. A sensitivity comparison asks how the answer changes under another plausible modeling decision. Their interpretation depends on who was counted, where, and over what period. A bridge vehicle counter, for example, measures a different population from residents commuting by car.
